01 Variables & Equations
🔤
Variables & Equations
Letters that hold numbers — the building block of every formula
BASIC

A variable is just a letter that stands for a number we don't know yet — or one that can change. You already use variables every day without realising it.

🛒
Real World Analogy
Imagine a price tag that says "Price = Cost + Profit". Here, Price, Cost, and Profit are all variables — they change depending on the product. In math we'd write this as P = C + R. When a shop buys a bag for £20 (C=20) and wants £5 profit (R=5), the price is P = 20 + 5 = £25. Simple — same thing in ML, just with more variables.
Score = 40 + 5 × StudyHours
Score is the variable we want to predict (output)  |  40 is a fixed starting value  |  5 is how much each study hour is worth  |  StudyHours is the input variable

02 Mean, Median & Mode
📊
Mean, Median & Mode
Three ways to find the "typical" value — each tells a different story
BASIC
💰
Real World Analogy
5 friends compare salaries: £25k, £28k, £30k, £32k, £200k. The billionaire friend's salary makes the Mean (average) = £63k — which represents nobody. The Median (middle value) = £30k — much more realistic. The Mode (most common) = none since all are different. In ML, always ask: is my mean being pulled by extreme values?
M
Mean (Average) — Add all, divide by count
Scores: 60, 70, 80, 90 → Mean = (60+70+80+90) ÷ 4 = 75. Excel: =AVERAGE(). Best when: no extreme outliers.
Md
Median — The exact middle value
Sort numbers, pick the middle one. Scores: 60, 70, 80, 90, 200 → Median = 80. Excel: =MEDIAN(). Best when: data is skewed or has outliers.
Mo
Mode — Most frequent value
Scores: 70, 80, 80, 90, 80, 60 → Mode = 80 (appears 3 times). Excel: =MODE.SNGL(). Best when: you want the "most common" answer.
Data: 30,000 28,000 30,000 32,000 200,000
Mean = (30,000+28,000+30,000+32,000+200,000) ÷ 5 = £64,000 ← Pulled up by outlier Median = Middle value when sorted = £30,000 ← Realistic typical salary Mode = £30,000 ← Appears twice
Lesson: When mean >> median → data is RIGHT-SKEWED → use median for ML preprocessing

Expressing parts of a whole — used in accuracy, pass rates, confusion matrices

04 Standard Deviation & Variance
📏
Standard Deviation
How spread out are the values? — essential for normalization and outlier detection
KEY CONCEPT
🎯
Real World Analogy
Two archers both average 8 points per shot. Archer A: scores 7, 8, 8, 9, 8 — very consistent, low spread. Archer B: scores 2, 10, 4, 15, 9 — same average but wildly inconsistent. Standard deviation measures this consistency — low std dev = reliable, high std dev = unpredictable. In ML, high std dev in a feature often means it needs scaling.
1
Find the Mean
Scores: 60, 70, 80, 90. Mean = (60+70+80+90) ÷ 4 = 75
2
Find each score's distance from the mean
60-75 = -15  |  70-75 = -5  |  80-75 = +5  |  90-75 = +15
3
Square each distance (remove negatives)
(-15)² = 225  |  (-5)² = 25  |  (5)² = 25  |  (15)² = 225
4
Average the squares = Variance
(225+25+25+225) ÷ 4 = 125 (This is the Variance)
5
Square root of variance = Standard Deviation
√125 ≈ 11.18. This means scores typically vary by about ±11 points from the average of 75.
Scores: 60 70 75(mean) 80 90 |←11→|←5→| · |←5→|←11→| Std Dev = 11.18 ← "Typical distance from the mean"
If std dev were 2: all scores very close to 75 (71-79) If std dev were 20: scores spread from 35 to 115
💡
In ML (Z-Score Normalization): We subtract the mean and divide by std dev. This makes every feature have mean=0 and std dev=1 — so no single feature dominates others just because its numbers are bigger. Excel: =STDEV.S(range)
✏️

05 Exponents & Square Roots
⚡
Exponents & Square Roots
Powers and reversal — used in distances, error calculations and sigmoid
BASIC
🦠
Real World Analogy
Exponent: A bacteria doubles every hour. Start with 1. After 3 hours: 2³ = 2×2×2 = 8. After 10 hours: 2¹⁰ = 1,024. The "power" tells you how many times to multiply. Square root: You know a square room's floor area is 64m². What's the side length? √64 = 8m. Square root is the "reverse" of squaring.
Exponents: 2² = 2×2 = 4 (Excel: =2^2) 3³ = 3×3×3 = 27 (Excel: =3^3) 5² = 5×5 = 25 (Excel: =5^2) Square Roots: √4 = 2 (Excel: =SQRT(4)) √25 = 5 (Excel: =SQRT(25)) √100 = 10 (Excel: =SQRT(100)) In ML — why we square first, then square root: Error = 5 → 5² = 25 (all positive, no negatives) Error = -5 → (-5)² = 25 (negative becomes positive!) Average of squared errors = Variance √Variance = Standard Deviation ← bring it back to original units
🎯
Why square errors in ML? If your model over-predicts by 10 and under-predicts by 10, they don't cancel out (which would falsely show 0 error). Squaring first makes both +10 and -10 become +100, so errors always add up honestly. Then we square-root at the end to get back to the original units.

06 The Straight Line — y = mx + b
📈
The Straight Line (y = mx + b)
The entire logic of linear regression — prediction is drawing a line through data
KEY CONCEPT
🚗
Real World Analogy
A taxi charges £3 flag-fall (base charge) + £2 per km. y = 2x + 3 where y = total cost, x = kilometres, 2 = cost per km (slope), 3 = base charge (intercept). Plot this on a graph — it's a perfect straight line. For 5km: y = 2(5)+3 = £13. For 10km: y = 2(10)+3 = £23. Linear regression does exactly this — finds the best straight line through your data points.
y = mx + b
y = what we predict (score, price, sales)  |  m = slope (effect per unit)  |  x = our input feature  |  b = intercept (starting value when x=0)
Taxi formula: Cost = 2 × km + 3 ML formula: Score = 4.2 × StudyHours + 42 km=0: Cost = 2×0 + 3 = £3 (just the base charge) km=5: Cost = 2×5 + 3 = £13 km=10: Cost = 2×10+ 3 = £23 StudyHours=0: Score = 4.2×0 + 42 = 42 (studies nothing, gets 42) StudyHours=5: Score = 4.2×5 + 42 = 63 StudyHours=8: Score = 4.2×8 + 42 = 75.6 Slope m=4.2 means: each extra study hour adds 4.2 exam points Intercept b=42 means: starting score if studying zero hours
Slope (m)
Steeper slope = bigger effect per unit. m=10 means 1 extra hour = 10 more points. m=0.5 means minimal effect.
In Day 6: SLOPE()
Intercept (b)
Where the line crosses the y-axis. The predicted value when all inputs = 0.
In Day 6: INTERCEPT()
Prediction
Plug any x value into the formula → get a predicted y. This is your model making a new prediction.
In Day 6: FORECAST()

07 Correlation
🔗
Correlation (r)
How strongly do two things move together? Ranges from -1 to +1
KEY CONCEPT
🌡️
Real World Analogy
Ice cream sales and temperature: When it's hot, more ice cream is sold. When it's cold, fewer sales. These move together in the same direction → positive correlation (r close to +1). Heating bills and temperature: When it's hot, heating bills drop. When cold, bills rise. Opposite directions → negative correlation (r close to -1). Shoe size and exam score: No relationship whatsoever → r close to 0.
r valueStrengthDirectionReal Example
+0.9 to +1.0Very Strong↑ Both riseHeight & weight
+0.5 to +0.9Moderate-Strong↑ Both riseStudy hours & score
0 to +0.3Weak↑ Barely togetherShoe size & salary
-0.5 to -0.9Moderate-Strong↓↑ OppositeAbsences & grade
-0.9 to -1.0Very Strong↓↑ OppositeSpeed & travel time
Study Hours vs Exam Score r = +0.82 (strong positive) StudyHours: 1 2 3 4 5 6 7 8 Score: 45 52 58 63 70 74 80 85 Pattern: As hours ↑, score ↑ → positive correlation Absences vs Exam Score r = -0.76 (strong negative) Absences: 0 1 2 3 5 7 9 12 Score: 88 82 78 74 65 58 50 40 Pattern: As absences ↑, score ↓ → negative correlation
💡
R² (R-squared) = r × r. If r=0.82, then R²=0.67. This means "study hours explains 67% of the variation in exam scores." The other 33% is explained by things we haven't measured (sleep, natural ability, stress etc.). Excel: =CORREL(col1, col2)

08 Probability
🎲
Probability (0 to 1)
How likely is something? — the output of every classification model
KEY CONCEPT
☔
Real World Analogy
A weather forecast says "70% chance of rain." That's a probability of 0.70 (or 70%). Probability always lives between 0 (impossible) and 1 (certain). 0.5 = 50/50. A logistic regression model outputs a probability: "This student has a 0.83 probability of passing." We then decide: if probability ≥ 0.5, predict Pass. If < 0.5, predict Fail. The 0.5 is called the threshold.
Probability scale: 0.0 ←─────────────────── 0.5 ───────────────────→ 1.0 Impossible 50/50 coin flip Certain Examples: Rolling a 6 on a dice: 1/6 = 0.167 (16.7%) Flipping heads on a coin: 1/2 = 0.500 (50%) Sun rising tomorrow: 1/1 = 1.000 (100%) Pig flying: 0/∞ = 0.000 (0%) In ML Classification: P(Pass) = 0.87 → Predict PASS ✓ (87% confident) P(Pass) = 0.43 → Predict FAIL ✗ (only 43% → below 0.5 threshold) P(Pass) = 0.51 → Predict PASS ✓ (just barely above threshold)

09 Logarithms
🔁
Logarithms (LN and LOG)
The reverse of exponents — used in log-loss (classifier training) and skew fixing
ADVANCED
📻
Real World Analogy
Sound is measured in decibels (dB) on a logarithmic scale. A whisper is 30dB, conversation 60dB, a concert 120dB. Going from 30→60 dB isn't twice as loud — it's 1,000 times louder. Logarithms compress huge number ranges into manageable ones. In ML, log is used to measure how wrong a confident-but-incorrect prediction is — being 99% confident when you're wrong is punished severely by the log function.
1
Exponent (power): "Start from 2, multiply 3 times"
2³ = 8. We start with 2, raise it to power 3, get 8.
2
Logarithm: "What power gives me 8?" (asking the reverse question)
log₂(8) = 3. "What power of 2 gives 8?" Answer: 3. So log is just the inverse of a power.
3
Natural Log (LN): uses base e ≈ 2.718
LN(1) = 0. LN(2.718) = 1. LN(0.5) = -0.69. LN of anything between 0 and 1 is negative — this is why log-loss is always negative of the log.
Why LN in classification loss: Actual = 1 (should pass). Model predicts: P = 0.99 → LN(0.99) = -0.01 (tiny loss — nearly right ✓) P = 0.70 → LN(0.70) = -0.36 (small loss — fairly confident) P = 0.50 → LN(0.50) = -0.69 (moderate — 50/50 is unhelpful) P = 0.10 → LN(0.10) = -2.30 (BIG loss — confidently wrong!) P = 0.01 → LN(0.01) = -4.61 (HUGE loss — very wrong and very confident) Log penalises confident wrong predictions much harder than uncertain ones. This teaches the model: "If you're going to be wrong, at least be uncertain."
💡
For fixing skewed data (Day 3): Income data like £15k, £20k, £18k, £500k is right-skewed. LN(15000)=9.6, LN(20000)=9.9, LN(18000)=9.8, LN(500000)=13.1. The outlier is still bigger but not catastrophically so — log compresses the scale. Excel: =LN(A1)
✏️

10 Euclidean Distance
📍
Euclidean Distance
How far apart are two points? — the heart of KNN and K-Means clustering
KEY CONCEPT
🗺️
Real World Analogy
You're at coordinate (2, 3) on a map. Your friend is at (5, 7). The straight-line distance between you (as the crow flies) is Euclidean distance. It's just Pythagoras theorem: distance = √((5-2)² + (7-3)²) = √(9+16) = √25 = 5 km. In ML, instead of map coordinates, the "coordinates" are feature values — like (StudyHours=4, Score=65).
Distance = √((x₁−x₂)² + (y₁−y₂)²)
x₁,y₁ = coordinates of point 1  |  x₂,y₂ = coordinates of point 2  |  Square → Add → Square Root
1
Point A: StudyHours=2, Score=55. Point B: StudyHours=6, Score=75.
We want to know: how "similar" are these two students?
2
Find the difference in each dimension
Δ StudyHours = 6−2 = 4. Δ Score = 75−55 = 20
3
Square each difference
4² = 16. 20² = 400
4
Add them and square root
√(16 + 400) = √416 ≈ 20.4 — the distance between Student A and Student B in feature space.
⚠️
Why scaling matters: If Score ranges 0–100 and StudyHours ranges 1–10, Score differences (e.g., 20) will dominate StudyHours differences (e.g., 4). Distance is mostly about Score and barely about StudyHours. After Min-Max scaling both to 0–1, they contribute equally. Always scale before computing distances!

11 The Dot Product (SUMPRODUCT)
⚡
The Dot Product
Multiply matching pairs, then add them up — this IS the prediction formula for ML
ADVANCED
🛍️
Real World Analogy
You bought: 3 apples at £0.50, 2 bananas at £0.30, 5 oranges at £0.80. Total = (3×0.50) + (2×0.30) + (5×0.80) = 1.50 + 0.60 + 4.00 = £6.10. You just computed a dot product! Items = features (3, 2, 5). Prices = weights (0.50, 0.30, 0.80). Total = prediction. Linear regression is exactly this — features × their learned coefficients, summed up.
Prediction = w₁×x₁ + w₂×x₂ + w₃×x₃
w = weights (coefficients the model learns)  |  x = feature values (your data)  |  Same as SUMPRODUCT(weights, features)
Shopping Dot Product: Items × Prices = Subtotals 3 × 0.50 = 1.50 2 × 0.30 = 0.60 5 × 0.80 = 4.00 ────── Total (dot product) = 6.10 ML Regression Prediction: Features × Coefficients = Contributions StudyHours=6 × β₁=4.2 = 25.2 Attendance=85 × β₂=0.31 = 26.35 Is_Eng=1 × β₃=6.8 = 6.80 Intercept = 42.1 ────── Predicted Score = 100.45 Excel SUMPRODUCT({6,85,1}, {4.2,0.31,6.8}) + 42.1 = same result

12 Summation (Σ)
Σ
Summation (The Sigma Symbol)
A shorthand for "add up a sequence of things" — seen everywhere in ML formulas
BASIC
🧾
Real World Analogy
Instead of writing "add up January sales + February sales + March sales + ... + December sales" we write Σ(monthly sales). The Σ (Greek letter Sigma) is just a fancy shorthand for "add everything up." When you see Σ in a formula, replace it mentally with "sum of all" or "=SUM()" in Excel. That's literally all it means.
Reading Sigma notation: Σ(xᵢ) = x₁ + x₂ + x₃ + ... + xₙ = SUM(all values) Mean formula written two ways: Mean = (x₁+x₂+...+xₙ) ÷ n (longhand) Mean = Σ(xᵢ) ÷ n (sigma shorthand) = AVERAGE(range) (Excel) RMSE (model error) formula: RMSE = √[ Σ(actual - predicted)² ÷ n ] = √[ SUM of all squared errors ÷ count ] = SQRT(SUMXMY2(actual,pred)/n) (Excel) WCSS in K-Means: WCSS = Σ(distance from each point to its centroid)² = SUMPRODUCT((cluster=k)*distance²) (Excel)

Enter 10 student records: Name, StudyHours (1–10), Attendance (50–100), Score (30–100). Convert to a Table named "Students".
2
Statistics: Calculate Mean, Median, Std Dev for Score. Is mean > median? (If yes → right skewed.)
3
Correlation: =CORREL(StudyHours, Score) and =CORREL(Attendance, Score). Which feature is a better predictor?
4
Straight Line: =SLOPE(Score, StudyHours) and =INTERCEPT(Score, StudyHours). Write the y=mx+b equation. Predict score for 7 study hours.
5
Probability (manual): Add a column P_Pass = IF(Score>=60, 1, 0). Count passes / 10 = pass rate (probability format 0–1).
6
Distance: Pick student 1 and student 2. Calculate Euclidean distance using their StudyHours and Score (scaled 0–1 first). Are they similar?
7
Dot Product: Use SUMPRODUCT to calculate each student's weighted score: (StudyHours × 0.4) + (Attendance × 0.006) — a simple hand-crafted "model."
