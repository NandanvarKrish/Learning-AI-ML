Bayes' Theorem

~2 hours · The formula behind Naive Bayes, spam filters & medical AI

01 Why this matters for AI/ML

Yesterday you saw that P(A|B) ≠ P(B|A). Bayes' Theorem is the formula that correctly converts one into the other. This single formula powers an entire ML algorithm (Naive Bayes, which you'll build by hand in Phase 3), and its core idea — updating a belief when new evidence arrives — is the philosophical foundation of how most ML models "learn" from data at all.

02 Deriving it from yesterday's formula

Recall from Day 10: P(A|B) = P(A and B) / P(B) and also P(B|A) = P(A and B) / P(A). Both equal the same joint probability P(A and B), just rearranged. Set them equal to each other and solve, and you get Bayes' Theorem:

P(A | B) = [ P(B | A) × P(A) ] / P(B)

This lets you calculate P(A|B) even when you only directly know P(B|A) — exactly the reversal problem from yesterday.

03 The vocabulary

Prior — P(A)
What you believed before seeing new evidence
Likelihood — P(B|A)
How probable the evidence is, if A is true
Posterior — P(A|B)
Your updated belief after seeing evidence B
Evidence — P(B)
Overall probability of observing B at all

The whole idea of Bayes' Theorem in one sentence: Posterior = (Likelihood × Prior) / Evidence. You start with a prior belief, see some evidence, and update to a posterior belief.

04 The classic worked example (medical testing)

The example every course uses — because it teaches the key lesson

A disease affects 1% of the population (prior). A test correctly detects it 90% of the time if you have it (likelihood, true positive rate), but also gives a false positive 5% of the time if you don't have it. You test positive. What's the actual probability you have the disease?

Most people guess ~90%. The real answer is much lower — let's calculate it using natural frequencies (often more intuitive than the formula):

Out of 10,000 people: 100 have the disease (1%), 9,900 don't.

Of the 100 with disease: 90 test positive (90% detection rate).

Of the 9,900 without disease: 5% false positive = 495 test positive anyway.

Total testing positive = 90 + 495 = 585. Of those, only 90 actually have the disease.

P(Disease | Positive) = 90 / 585 ≈ 0.154 (about 15.4%)

Even with a "90% accurate" test, a positive result only means ~15% real probability of disease — because the disease is rare (low prior), false positives from the huge healthy population overwhelm the true positives from the tiny sick population. This is called the base rate fallacy when people ignore it, and it's one of the most important lessons in all of applied probability.

05 Second example (spam filter, reversed from Day 10)

Reconnecting to yesterday's spam table

Suppose you only know: P(Spam) = 0.2 (prior), P("free" | Spam) = 0.9 (likelihood), and P("free" | Not Spam) = 0.075 (from Day 10's table: 60/800). Find P(Spam | "free") using Bayes:

First find P("free") overall = P("free"|Spam)×P(Spam) + P("free"|NotSpam)×P(NotSpam) = (0.9×0.2) + (0.075×0.8) = 0.18 + 0.06 = 0.24

P(Spam | "free") = (0.9 × 0.2) / 0.24 = 0.18 / 0.24 = 0.75

This matches exactly what you calculated directly from the table yesterday (180/240 = 0.75) — Bayes' Theorem just lets you get there even when you don't have the full table, only the prior and likelihoods. This is precisely how a real Naive Bayes spam classifier works internally.

06 Common mistakes to avoid

Mistake 1 — Ignoring the prior (base rate fallacy). The medical example above is the textbook case — a rare condition means even a fairly accurate test produces mostly false alarms. Always factor in how common something is.

Mistake 2 — Forgetting to calculate the full "evidence" term P(B). You need BOTH P(B|A) and P(B|not A) to correctly compute P(B) using the law of total probability shown above.

Mistake 3 — Treating posterior as certainty. A posterior of 0.75 still means 25% chance you're wrong — Bayes' Theorem gives you an updated probability, not a guarantee.

Mistake 4 — Assuming intuition beats the math. As the medical test example shows, gut feeling badly misjudges these problems — always compute it out, especially in low-prior situations.
