Scatter Plots & Histograms

~2 hours · Relationships between variables, and the shape of a single one

01 Why this matters for AI/ML

These two chart types answer the two most common questions you'll have about a dataset. A scatter plot asks: "are these two variables related?" — which is exactly the correlation question from Day 13, now visual. A histogram asks: "what shape is this data?" — exactly the distribution question from Day 8, now visual. Both are standard, everyday tools in real analysis.

02 Scatter plots — two variables at once

import matplotlib.pyplot as plt hours_studied = [1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8] exam_score = [35, 42, 50, 58, 67, 72, 81, 88] plt.scatter(hours_studied, exam_score) plt.xlabel("Hours Studied") plt.ylabel("Exam Score") plt.title("Study Time vs Score") plt.show()

Each dot is one data point plotted at its (x, y) position. Unlike a line chart, points are NOT connected — which is correct here, because these are independent observations, not a sequence over time.

03 Reading a scatter plot (reconnects Day 13 and Day 20)

Upward-sloping cloud of points → positive correlation (as x rises, y tends to rise)

Downward-sloping cloud → negative correlation

Shapeless blob with no direction → little to no linear correlation

And the crucial Day 13 caveat, restated: a strong visual trend in a scatter plot shows CORRELATION only. It never proves that x causes y — a confounding variable or reverse causation could equally explain the pattern.

04 Histograms — the shape of one variable

ages = [22, 25, 25, 27, 28, 28, 28, 30, 31, 33, 34, 35, 38, 41, 45] plt.hist(ages, bins=5) plt.xlabel("Age") plt.ylabel("Number of people") plt.title("Age Distribution") plt.show()

A histogram groups values into ranges ("bins") and shows how many values fall in each — this is literally Day 8's distribution concept, drawn. The bins parameter matters a lot: too few bins hides detail, too many makes the shape noisy and hard to read. Adjusting bins and re-looking is a normal, expected part of exploring data.

05 What a histogram tells you (reconnects Day 8)

Every shape from Day 8, now recognizable at a glance

Symmetric bell shape → roughly normal distribution (mean ≈ median)

Long tail stretching right → right-skewed (mean pulled above median) — income, house prices

Long tail stretching left → left-skewed

Two separate humps → possibly two distinct subgroups mixed into one dataset, worth investigating

This is why plotting a histogram is often more informative than just reading .describe() — the shape reveals things that mean and standard deviation alone can hide entirely.

06 Common mistakes to avoid

Mistake 1 — Reading causation into a scatter plot. A clear upward trend means correlation, not cause — Day 13's entire lesson applies directly here.

Mistake 2 — Using a default bin count without checking alternatives. The same data can look normal or bimodal depending on bin choice — always try a couple of values.

Mistake 3 — Using a scatter plot where a line chart fits better. For sequential/time data, a line chart shows the progression more clearly; scatter suits independent paired observations.

Mistake 4 — Plotting a histogram of a categorical column. Histograms bin NUMERIC ranges — for categories, use a bar chart of .value_counts() instead.
