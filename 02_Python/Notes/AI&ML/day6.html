Mean, Median & Mode

~2 hours · First day of Statistics

01 Why this matters for AI/ML

Before you train any model, you always look at your data first — and mean, median, and mode are the very first tools for understanding what a dataset "typically" looks like. Data scientists spend real time just summarizing data before modeling. You'll also use these constantly to detect problems: outliers, skewed data, missing value handling — all decisions rooted in today's three numbers.

02 Mean (the average)

The mean is what most people call "the average" — add up all values, divide by how many there are:

Mean = (sum of all values) / (number of values)

Example: A small shop's daily sales this week (in ₹'000): 12, 15, 14, 60, 13

Mean = (12+15+14+60+13) / 5 = 114 / 5 = 22.8

Notice something odd — most days were around ₹12–15k, but the mean says ₹22.8k. That's because one unusual day (₹60k, maybe a bulk order) pulled the average upward. This is your first hint of why mean alone can mislead.

03 Median (the middle value)

The median is the value exactly in the middle when data is sorted. It ignores extreme values entirely.

Steps: 1) Sort the data. 2) If odd count, the median is the middle number. 3) If even count, average the two middle numbers.

Same shop data, sorted: 12, 13, 14, 15, 60 → middle value (3rd of 5) = 14

Compare: Mean = 22.8, Median = 14. The median (₹14k) is a much more honest picture of a "typical day" — the mean got dragged up by one outlier.

What if there's an even number of values?

Data: 10, 12, 14, 18 (sorted, 4 values). The two middle values are 12 and 14. Median = (12+14)/2 = 13.

04 Mode (the most frequent value)

The mode is simply the value that appears most often in a dataset. Unlike mean/median, a dataset can have no mode (all values unique), one mode, or multiple modes (tied frequencies).

Example: Shoe sizes sold today: 7, 8, 8, 9, 8, 10, 7

8 appears 3 times (most frequent) → Mode = 8

This is especially useful for categorical data — like "most common product size" or "most common customer complaint type" — where mean and median don't even make sense to calculate.

05 Real-world example (salary data — the classic case)

The example every stats course uses, for good reason

A small company's monthly salaries (in ₹'000): 25, 28, 27, 26, 30, 250 (the owner/CEO)

Mean = (25+28+27+26+30+250)/6 = 386/6 ≈ ₹64.3k

Median (sorted: 25,26,27,28,30,250 — average of 3rd & 4th) = (27+28)/2 = ₹27.5k

If a job ad said "average salary ₹64.3k," it would be technically true but wildly misleading — nearly every employee earns close to ₹27k. This is exactly why median is often reported for income data (you may have seen "median household income" in the news) — it resists distortion from a few extreme outliers.

06 When to use which

Use Mean when data is fairly evenly spread with no extreme outliers (e.g., average test score in a normal class).

Use Median when data has outliers or is skewed (income, house prices, company sizes).

Use Mode for categorical data, or to find the "most common" case (best-selling product size, most common customer age group).

07 Common mistakes to avoid

Mistake 1 — Finding the median without sorting first. The median only works on sorted data. Skipping this step gives a meaningless number.

Mistake 2 — Forgetting to average the two middle values for even-count data. Many people just pick one of the two middles — always average both.

Mistake 3 — Assuming every dataset has exactly one mode. Some datasets have no repeats (no mode), others have several values tied for most frequent (multiple modes).

Mistake 4 — Always trusting the mean. As you saw with salaries, mean can badly mislead when outliers exist. Always check median too, especially for financial or population data.
