Day 1 of 210 · Phase 1: Math Foundations

Numbers, Variables & Linear Equations

~2 hours · No prior math background needed

01 Why this matters for AI/ML

Almost every ML model — even the fancy ones — is built on one idea you'll learn today: an equation with variables that connects inputs to outputs. When you later learn "linear regression," you're really just learning a smarter version of solving y = mx + c. Today you build the foundation for that.

02 What is a "number" in this context?

You already know numbers from daily life — prices, marks, dates. In math, we group them:

Natural numbers: 1, 2, 3, 4... (counting things)

Whole numbers: 0, 1, 2, 3... (natural numbers + zero)

Integers: ...-2, -1, 0, 1, 2... (includes negatives — e.g., a loss of ₹500 is -500)

Rational numbers: anything expressible as a fraction — 0.5, 3/4, 2.25

In ML, almost everything you touch — prices, pixel values, probabilities — is one of these. You don't need to memorize the categories; just recognize that negative numbers and decimals are completely normal in data.

03 What is a variable?

A variable is a placeholder for an unknown or changing value — usually written as a letter like x, y, or n.

Everyday example

If a shopkeeper says "I sell shirts for ₹x each," then x stands in for whatever price he chooses — ₹500, ₹800, anything. The letter doesn't change; the value it represents does.

In ML, variables represent things like "number of years of experience," "size of a house in sq. ft," or "temperature." The model's whole job is to figure out how one variable affects another.

04 What is a linear equation?

A linear equation is a statement that says two expressions are equal, where the variable appears only to the power of 1 (no x², no √x). The general form:

ax + b = c

Here, a, b, c are known numbers, and x is the unknown you're solving for. "Solving" means isolating x on one side.

See the solving steps worked out

Solve: 3x + 6 = 21

Step 1: Subtract 6 from both sides → 3x = 15

Step 2: Divide both sides by 3 → x = 5

Check: 3(5) + 6 = 15 + 6 = 21 ✓

05 Real-world example (business context)

Break-even analysis — a concept you already know from commerce

Suppose you're starting a small business selling handmade candles.

  • Fixed cost (rent, equipment): ₹4,000
  • Cost to make each candle: ₹40
  • You sell each candle for: ₹90

Let x = number of candles sold. Your profit equation is:

Profit = 90x − (4000 + 40x)

You want to know: how many candles must you sell to break even (Profit = 0)?

0 = 90x − 4000 − 40x

Simplify: 0 = 50x − 4000 → 50x = 4000 → x = 80

You need to sell 80 candles to break even. This exact kind of equation — reducing a real business question to ax + b = c — is precisely the skill ML regression automates at scale, just with thousands of data points instead of one candle shop.

05b Second real-world example (salary/commission)

Sales commission — another everyday business scenario

Say you take a sales job with a fixed monthly salary of ₹15,000 plus a commission of ₹200 per sale. You want to know: how many sales do you need to earn ₹35,000 this month?

Let x = number of sales. Your total earning equation:

15000 + 200x = 35000
See the solving steps

Step 1: Subtract 15000 from both sides → 200x = 20000

Step 2: Divide both sides by 200 → x = 100

Answer: You need 100 sales this month.

Notice the pattern: fixed amount + (rate × unknown) = target. This exact structure — a fixed part plus a rate multiplied by an unknown — is the backbone of linear regression, which you'll formally meet in Phase 3.

05c Common mistakes to avoid

Mistake 1 — Forgetting to do the same operation to both sides. If you subtract 6 from the left side, you must subtract 6 from the right side too, or the equation is no longer balanced.

Mistake 2 — Sign errors with negatives. `3x − 6 = 21` is not the same as `3x + 6 = 21`. Always double-check the sign before moving a term across the equals sign.

Mistake 3 — Dividing before combining like terms. In `90x − 40x − 4000 = 0`, combine `90x − 40x = 50x` first, then handle the rest — don't divide early.

Mistake 4 — Not checking your answer. Always plug your value of x back into the original equation. If both sides don't match, you made an arithmetic slip somewhere.
