Matrix Multiplication

~2 hours · The single most important computation in deep learning

01 Why this matters for AI/ML

Every time a neural network processes data, it's performing matrix multiplication — inputs (a matrix) multiplied by weights (another matrix), layer after layer. GPUs exist largely because they're extremely good at doing matrix multiplication in parallel. This is, without exaggeration, the single most repeated calculation in all of modern AI.

02 The dimension rule

To multiply matrix A by matrix B, the number of columns in A must equal the number of rows in B. If A is m×n and B is n×p, the result is an m×p matrix:

(m × n) × (n × p) = (m × p)

The "inner" numbers (n and n) must match — they cancel out, leaving the "outer" numbers (m and p) as the result's shape. If they don't match, the multiplication is simply undefined — not "wrong," just impossible.

03 How each element is calculated

Each element of the result is found by taking the dot product of a row from A and a column from B — multiply corresponding entries, then sum them:

Worked example:

A = [[1,2],[3,4]]   B = [[5,6],[7,8]]

Result[1,1] = (row 1 of A) · (column 1 of B) = (1×5) + (2×7) = 5+14 = 19

Result[1,2] = (row 1 of A) · (column 2 of B) = (1×6) + (2×8) = 6+16 = 24

Result[2,1] = (row 2 of A) · (column 1 of B) = (3×5) + (4×7) = 15+28 = 43

Result[2,2] = (row 2 of A) · (column 2 of B) = (3×6) + (4×8) = 18+32 = 50

Final: A × B = [[19, 24], [43, 50]]

The pattern in words

To get the element at Result[row i, column j]: walk along row i of A and down column j of B simultaneously, multiplying paired numbers and adding up all the products. This is exactly the "dot product" operation, applied repeatedly for every row/column combination.

04 Real-world example (revenue calculation)

Business example — a genuinely useful real calculation

A company sells 2 products across 2 stores. Quantities sold (Stores × Products):

Q = [[10, 5], [8, 12]] (Store 1: 10 of Product A, 5 of Product B. Store 2: 8 of A, 12 of B.)

Prices (Products × 1): P = [[200], [150]] (Product A = ₹200, Product B = ₹150)

Revenue per store = Q × P:

Store 1 revenue = (10×200) + (5×150) = 2000+750 = ₹2,750

Store 2 revenue = (8×200) + (12×150) = 1600+1800 = ₹3,400

This exact calculation — a quantities matrix multiplied by a price vector to get total revenue — is a genuinely standard real-world use of matrix multiplication, no ML required at all.

05 Second example (weighted scoring)

Preview of how a neuron computes its output

A student's scores in 3 subjects: S = [80, 90, 70] (Math, Science, English). Weights for a combined index: W = [0.5, 0.3, 0.2] (Math counts most, English least).

Weighted score = (80×0.5) + (90×0.3) + (70×0.2) = 40+27+14 = 81

This is precisely what a single artificial neuron computes: multiply each input by its corresponding weight, then sum. You just did, by hand, the core operation inside every neural network you'll ever build.

06 Common mistakes to avoid

Mistake 1 — Assuming matrix multiplication is commutative. In general, A×B ≠ B×A — order matters enormously (unlike regular number multiplication).

Mistake 2 — Confusing this with element-wise multiplication. Simply multiplying matching positions together (like vector addition's pattern) is a DIFFERENT, less common operation — true matrix multiplication uses the row-times-column dot product method shown above.

Mistake 3 — Ignoring the dimension rule. Always check inner dimensions match before attempting multiplication — if A is 2×3 and B is 2×2, A×B is simply undefined.

Mistake 4 — Losing track of which row/column you're on. With larger matrices, it's easy to lose your place — work through one result cell completely before starting the next.
