Variance & Standard Deviation

~2 hours · Builds directly on Day 6's mean/median

01 Why this matters for AI/ML

Knowing the mean tells you the center of your data. It tells you nothing about how spread out the data is — and that spread matters enormously in ML. Standard deviation is used to scale features before training (so one column with huge numbers doesn't dominate another), to detect outliers, and to describe the "normal distribution" you'll meet very soon. It's one of the most-used numbers in all of data science.

02 The problem variance solves

Two classes both scored a mean of 70 on a test:

Class A scores: 68, 69, 70, 71, 72 — everyone close to 70

Class B scores: 20, 50, 70, 90, 120 — wildly spread out, but the mean is still 70

Same mean, completely different story. Variance and standard deviation are what let you tell these two situations apart numerically.

03 What is variance?

Variance measures the average "squared distance" of each data point from the mean:

Variance = Σ(x − mean)² / n

Read this as: for each value, subtract the mean, square the result (to make it positive), add all of those up, then divide by how many values there are.

Worked example step by step

Data: 4, 8, 6, 10, 12. Mean = (4+8+6+10+12)/5 = 40/5 = 8

Variance = 40 / 5 = 8

Why square the deviations instead of just adding them? Because positive and negative deviations would cancel out to zero every single time (that's a mathematical property of the mean) — squaring forces everything positive so the spread doesn't vanish in the math.

04 What is standard deviation?

Standard deviation is simply the square root of variance:

Standard Deviation = √Variance

For the example above: √8 ≈ 2.83. Why bother taking the square root? Because variance is in squared units (e.g., "rupees squared," which means nothing intuitively), while standard deviation brings it back to the original unit (rupees, marks, kg) — making it directly interpretable and comparable to the actual data.

05 Real-world example (investment risk)

Finance example — std dev as "risk"

Two mutual funds both average 12% annual return over 5 years:

Fund A yearly returns: 11%, 12%, 13%, 11%, 13% — mean = 12%, low spread

Fund B yearly returns: -10%, 30%, 5%, 25%, 10% — mean = 12%, high spread

Both funds have the identical average return, but Fund B swings wildly year to year — that's higher risk. In finance, standard deviation of returns literally is the standard measure of investment risk/volatility. If you've heard "high-risk, high-reward" investments described that way, standard deviation is the number behind that label.

06 A quick note: population vs. sample

You may later see variance divided by n (population variance — used when you have ALL the data) versus n − 1 (sample variance — used when your data is just a sample representing a larger group). Most real-world data science uses n − 1 (called Bessel's correction) because you're almost always working with a sample, not the entire population. Don't worry about proving why — just recognize both formulas exist, and libraries like Pandas default to the n − 1 version.

07 Common mistakes to avoid

Mistake 1 — Forgetting to square the deviations. Without squaring, positive and negative deviations cancel to exactly zero, giving a useless "average deviation" of 0 every time.

Mistake 2 — Comparing variance directly to your data. Variance is in squared units — don't compare a variance of "64" to data that ranges from 0–20 and conclude something is wildly off. Convert to standard deviation (√64 = 8) first.

Mistake 3 — Confusing high variance with "bad" data. High variance just means more spread — it's not inherently good or bad, it depends on context (low spread is good for consistent product quality, but might mean "boring" for stock returns some investors want).

Mistake 4 — Skipping the mean calculation. You cannot compute variance without first finding the mean — always calculate it as step one.
