Data Distributions & the Normal Curve

~2 hours · Builds on Day 6–7's mean, median & std dev

01 Why this matters for AI/ML

Many ML techniques quietly assume your data looks like a "normal distribution" — the classic bell curve. Linear regression's error assumptions, z-score standardization (which you previewed on Day 7), anomaly detection, and even how neural network weights get initialized all lean on this shape. Today you learn to recognize it — and just as importantly, recognize when your data does not look like it.

02 What is a distribution?

A distribution shows how often each value (or range of values) occurs in your data. The most common way to see this is a histogram — bars showing how many data points fall into each range.

Example: Marks of 20 students, grouped into ranges:

0–20: 1 student · 20–40: 2 students · 40–60: 5 students · 60–80: 8 students · 80–100: 4 students

Plotted as bars, this shape tells you at a glance where most students scored — here, clustered in the 60–80 range, tapering off on both sides.

03 The normal distribution (bell curve)

A normal distribution is a specific, very common shape: symmetric, bell-shaped, with most values clustered near the mean and fewer values as you move away in either direction.

Key property: in a perfect normal distribution, mean = median = mode — all three land at the exact center of the bell.

Classic real-world examples: adult human heights, blood pressure readings, measurement errors, IQ scores — many natural phenomena settle into this shape when you have enough data.

04 The 68-95-99.7 rule

This is the single most useful fact about normal distributions, and it connects directly to Day 7's standard deviation:

68% of data falls within 1 standard deviation of the mean

95% of data falls within 2 standard deviations of the mean

99.7% of data falls within 3 standard deviations of the mean

Worked example

Adult height: mean = 165cm, std dev = 7cm (roughly, for illustration).

68% of people are between 158cm and 172cm (165 ± 7)

95% of people are between 151cm and 179cm (165 ± 14)

Someone at 190cm would be about 3.5 std devs away — genuinely rare, which matches real life.

This rule is exactly why standard deviation is so powerful: once you know mean and std dev, you can estimate how "normal" or "unusual" any specific value is — the foundation of outlier and anomaly detection.

05 Skewed distributions (when data is NOT symmetric)

Symmetric (Normal)
Bell-shaped, mean = median
Right-Skewed
Long tail to the right, mean > median
Left-Skewed
Long tail to the left, mean < median

Reconnecting to Day 6's salary example

Remember the company salaries: 25, 28, 27, 26, 30, 250 (₹'000)? Mean = 64.3, Median = 27.5. Mean sits far to the right of the median — that's the signature of a right-skewed distribution: most values are low/moderate, but a long tail of high earners drags the mean upward. Income, house prices, and company revenues are almost always right-skewed in real life — this is precisely why median is preferred for reporting them.

06 Common mistakes to avoid

Mistake 1 — Assuming all real-world data is normally distributed. It often isn't — income, city population sizes, and website traffic are usually skewed, not bell-shaped.

Mistake 2 — Mixing up skew direction. The skew is named after which side the tail stretches toward, not where most of the data sits. A long tail to the right = right-skewed, even though most data might sit on the left.

Mistake 3 — Applying the 68-95-99.7 rule to non-normal data. This rule only holds for (approximately) normal distributions — applying it to heavily skewed data gives wrong estimates.

Mistake 4 — Confusing "distribution" with just "range." Range only tells you min and max; distribution tells you the full shape of how data is spread between them.
