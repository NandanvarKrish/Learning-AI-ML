The Dot Product

~2 hours · One number that captures how "aligned" two vectors are

01 Why this matters for AI/ML

You already computed dot products on Day 19 without naming them — every "row times column" step in matrix multiplication IS a dot product. Today you isolate this operation on its own, because it has a second life: measuring how similar two vectors are — the exact math behind recommendation systems, search relevance, and word embeddings (which represent word meaning as vectors).

02 The algebraic definition

The dot product of two same-length vectors is: multiply corresponding components, then add everything up. The result is always a single number (a scalar), never another vector:

a · b = a₁b₁ + a₂b₂ + ... + aₙbₙ

Example: a = [2, 3], b = [4, 1]

a · b = (2×4) + (3×1) = 8 + 3 = 11

03 The geometric meaning

There's a second formula for the same dot product, connecting it to angles:

a · b = |a| × |b| × cos(θ)

Where |a| and |b| are the magnitudes (Day 16) and θ (theta) is the angle between the two vectors. Both formulas always give the identical number — one is computed from components, the other from geometry.

Dot Product > 0
Vectors point in a generally similar direction (angle < 90°)
Dot Product = 0
Vectors are perpendicular / orthogonal (angle = 90°)
Dot Product < 0
Vectors point in generally opposite directions (angle > 90°)

This is the single most useful takeaway from today: the sign of a dot product tells you, at a glance, whether two vectors "agree" in direction.

04 Real-world example (recommendation systems)

The example that makes "similarity" concrete

Two customers' genre preference vectors (rated 0–5 for Action, Comedy, Romance):

Customer A: [5, 1, 0] (loves action, dislikes comedy/romance)

Customer B: [4, 2, 1] (also mostly likes action)

Customer C: [0, 1, 5] (loves romance, dislikes action)

A · B = (5×4)+(1×2)+(0×1) = 20+2+0 = 22 (high — similar taste)

A · C = (5×0)+(1×1)+(0×5) = 0+1+0 = 1 (very low — different taste)

A recommendation engine would confidently suggest "customers similar to A also liked..." using Customer B's other watched movies, but NOT Customer C's — this dot-product-based similarity scoring is genuinely how early recommendation systems worked, and still underlies parts of modern ones.

05 Second example (perpendicular = unrelated)

Understanding orthogonality

Two feature vectors: "hours spent gaming" = [1, 0] and "hours spent gardening" = [0, 1] (using some 2-feature simplified space). Their dot product = (1×0)+(0×1) = 0.

A dot product of exactly 0 means these two directions are completely independent of each other — knowing one tells you nothing about the other. This "orthogonal = unrelated" intuition becomes important later when you learn about feature independence and dimensionality reduction (PCA, in Phase 3).

06 Common mistakes to avoid

Mistake 1 — Forgetting the dot product requires matching vector lengths. Just like vector addition, you can't dot a 2D vector with a 3D vector.

Mistake 2 — Expecting a vector as the result. The dot product always returns a single scalar number, never another vector — this trips people up coming from matrix multiplication where the result is often still a matrix.

Mistake 3 — Misreading the sign. A large positive number means strong alignment; it does NOT necessarily mean the vectors are identical — only that they point in a broadly similar direction.

Mistake 4 — Confusing raw dot product magnitude with "similarity." A longer vector can produce a bigger dot product even at the same angle — this is exactly why "cosine similarity" (dot product divided by both magnitudes) is often used instead in ML, to normalize for vector length.
