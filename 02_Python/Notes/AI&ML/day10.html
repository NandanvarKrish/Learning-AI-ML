Conditional Probability

~2 hours · Sets up Bayes' Theorem tomorrow — pay close attention today

01 Why this matters for AI/ML

Spam filters, medical diagnosis models, and recommendation engines all answer some version of: "given that I already know X happened, what's the probability Y also happens?" That's exactly what conditional probability formalizes. It's also the single building block behind Naive Bayes, an entire classification algorithm you'll build from scratch in Phase 3.

02 What is conditional probability?

Conditional probability asks: given that event B has already happened, what's the probability of event A? Written as:

P(A | B) = P(A and B) / P(B)

Read "P(A | B)" as "the probability of A, given B." The key idea: knowing B happened shrinks your sample space — you're no longer looking at all possible outcomes, only the ones where B is true.

03 Worked example (email spam)

1,000 emails, categorized:

Contains "free"Doesn't contain "free"Total
Spam18020200
Not spam60740800
Total2407601000

Question: Given an email contains the word "free," what's the probability it's spam? P(Spam | "free")

We restrict our attention ONLY to the "contains free" column (240 emails total) — that's our new, shrunk sample space.

P(Spam | "free") = 180 / 240 = 0.75

Compare this to the overall P(Spam) = 200/1000 = 0.2 — knowing the email contains "free" massively increases the estimated spam probability, from 20% to 75%. That shift is the entire point of conditional probability.

04 Real-world example (cross-selling)

Business example — market basket analysis

A store's transaction data over 500 purchases:

Bought BreadDidn't buy BreadTotal
Bought Butter15030180
Didn't buy Butter50270320
Total200300500

P(Butter | Bread) = 150/200 = 0.75 — 75% of customers who buy bread also buy butter. This is exactly the logic behind "customers who bought this also bought..." recommendations you see on e-commerce sites — it's conditional probability computed at massive scale across millions of transactions.

05 The order matters — P(A|B) ≠ P(B|A)

From the spam table: P(Spam | "free") = 0.75 (75% of "free" emails are spam).

But P("free" | Spam) = 180/200 = 0.9 (90% of spam emails contain "free").

These are two completely different numbers answering two completely different questions. This exact confusion — mixing up P(A|B) with P(B|A) — is the single most common mistake in probability, and it's precisely what tomorrow's Bayes' Theorem exists to correctly untangle.

06 Independence

Two events are independent if knowing one happened tells you nothing about the other — mathematically, P(A|B) = P(A).

Example: Does the weather today affect a coin flip result? No — P(Heads | Raining) = P(Heads) = 0.5. Weather and coin flips are independent.

Compare this to the bread/butter example: P(Butter | Bread) = 0.75, while overall P(Butter) = 180/500 = 0.36. Since these differ, buying bread and buying butter are NOT independent — they're related purchase behaviors.

07 Common mistakes to avoid

Mistake 1 — Confusing P(A|B) with P(B|A). Always double-check which event is the "given" condition — it changes the denominator entirely.

Mistake 2 — Using the full total instead of the restricted total. Once you condition on B, your denominator becomes the count of B only, not the grand total.

Mistake 3 — Assuming independence without checking. Many real-world events look independent but aren't (or vice versa) — always compare P(A|B) to P(A) before assuming.

Mistake 4 — Forgetting conditional probability requires P(B) > 0. You can't condition on an event that never happens — the formula breaks (division by zero).
