What Is a Vector?

~2 hours · Pair today with 3Blue1Brown's "Essence of Linear Algebra" Ep. 1
Companion video: Watch 3Blue1Brown — "Vectors, what even are they?" (~10 min) either before or after this page — the visual animation of vectors as arrows is genuinely one of the best explanations available anywhere, free.

01 Why this matters for AI/ML

Every single piece of data an ML model touches — a customer's age and income, the pixels in a photo, the words in a sentence — gets converted into a vector before the model can use it. Understanding what a vector actually is, geometrically, makes concepts like "distance between two data points" and "similarity" click immediately instead of feeling abstract.

02 What is a vector?

A vector is an ordered list of numbers that can represent both a direction and a magnitude (length). Written as:

v = [3, 4]

Geometrically, this is an arrow starting at the origin (0,0) and pointing to the point (3,4) — 3 steps right, 4 steps up. The two numbers are called components.

Vector vs. point — an important distinction: the point (3,4) is just a location. The vector [3,4] is the arrow from the origin to that location — it carries both "how far" and "which direction." In practice, especially in ML, we often use them interchangeably, but the arrow framing is what makes vector math (adding, scaling) intuitive.

03 Vectors beyond 2D

Nothing stops a vector from having more than 2 numbers — you just can't easily draw it anymore past 3 dimensions. A vector with 5 numbers, like [25, 50000, 3, 1, 0], might represent a customer's age, income, number of purchases, membership status, and something else — 5 dimensions, one vector, even though you can't sketch it on paper.

This is the key mental leap

In ML, a single row of your dataset — one customer, one house, one email — is a vector. The "columns" of your dataset are the vector's components. When you have thousands of rows, you have thousands of vectors — and the whole dataset together is called a matrix (tomorrow's topic).

04 Real-world example (customer as a vector)

Business example

An e-commerce company describes each customer using 3 numbers: age, average order value (₹), and number of orders this year.

Customer A: [28, 1200, 15] — 28 years old, ₹1,200 average order, 15 orders

Customer B: [45, 3000, 3] — 45 years old, ₹3,000 average order, 3 orders

These are 3-dimensional vectors. You can't draw them on paper easily, but the computer can still measure how "close" or "far apart" these two customers are — the exact technique behind customer segmentation, which you'll build for real in Phase 3.

05 Magnitude (length of a vector)

The magnitude of a 2D vector [x, y] is its length — the straight-line distance from the origin, calculated using the Pythagorean theorem:

|v| = √(x² + y²)

Example: for v = [3, 4]: |v| = √(3² + 4²) = √(9+16) = √25 = 5

This generalizes to any number of dimensions — for [x, y, z]: |v| = √(x²+y²+z²), and so on. This exact formula is used constantly in ML to measure "distance" between data points (e.g., in K-Nearest Neighbors, which you'll build in Phase 3).

06 Common mistakes to avoid

Mistake 1 — Ignoring the order of components. [3, 4] and [4, 3] are different vectors — order matters, and each position means something specific (e.g., position 1 = age, position 2 = income).

Mistake 2 — Confusing magnitude with a component. The magnitude |v| = 5 for [3,4] is NOT one of the vector's numbers — it's a separately calculated single number describing overall length.

Mistake 3 — Assuming vectors must be 2D or 3D to make sense. A 100-dimensional vector is mathematically completely valid, even though you can never draw it — the arithmetic (magnitude, addition) works exactly the same way.

Mistake 4 — Forgetting vectors need matching dimensions to compare/combine. You can't meaningfully add a 2D vector to a 3D vector — they need the same number of components (this becomes very relevant once you reach vector addition tomorrow).
