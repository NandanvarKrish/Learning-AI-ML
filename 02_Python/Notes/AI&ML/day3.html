Slope, Intercept & Graphs of Lines

~2 hours · Builds directly on Day 2's plotter

01 Why this matters for AI/ML

Remember yesterday's f(x) = mx + c? Today you learn exactly what m and c mean geometrically — and in ML, these get renamed weight (m) and bias (c). Every single neural network parameter you'll ever train is, conceptually, one of these two things repeated millions of times. Understanding slope and intercept deeply today makes "weights and biases" feel obvious later instead of mysterious.

02 What is slope?

Slope measures how steep a line is — how much y changes for every 1 unit increase in x. The formula, given two points (x₁,y₁) and (x₂,y₂):

m = (y₂ − y₁) / (x₂ − x₁)

People remember this as "rise over run" — rise (vertical change) divided by run (horizontal change).

Worked example: Find the slope between (1, 3) and (4, 9).

m = (9 − 3) / (4 − 1) = 6 / 3 = 2

This means: for every 1 step to the right, the line rises 2 steps up.

What different slope values look like

Positive slope (m > 0): line rises left to right — as x increases, y increases.

Negative slope (m < 0): line falls left to right — as x increases, y decreases.

Zero slope (m = 0): perfectly flat horizontal line — y never changes.

Larger |m|: steeper line. Smaller |m|: flatter line.

03 What is the intercept?

The y-intercept (c) is where the line crosses the y-axis — the value of y when x = 0. In f(x) = mx + c, plug in x = 0: f(0) = m(0) + c = c. That's why c alone tells you the starting point of the line before x has any effect.

04 Real-world example (asset depreciation — straight-line method)

Commerce example — you may already know this term

A company buys a machine for ₹1,00,000. Using the straight-line depreciation method, its value drops by ₹8,000 per year. The machine's value as a function of years (x):

Value(x) = 100000 − 8000x

Here, slope (m) = −8000 (value falls by 8000 each year — negative slope, sloping downward), and intercept (c) = 100000 (the starting value at year 0, before any depreciation).

After 5 years: Value(5) = 100000 − 8000(5) = 100000 − 40000 = ₹60,000. If you've studied depreciation schedules in commerce, you've already been reading slope-intercept graphs without the ML vocabulary attached to them.

05 Second real-world example (taxi fare)

Everyday example

A taxi charges a base fare of ₹50 plus ₹12 per km. Fare as a function of distance (x, in km):

Fare(x) = 12x + 50

Slope = 12 (rate per km, positive — fare rises with distance). Intercept = 50 (what you'd pay even for 0 km — the base charge). Compare this to yesterday's recharge plan and Day 1's break-even example — same structure, three different real contexts. That repetition is intentional: this pattern is genuinely everywhere.

06 Common mistakes to avoid

Mistake 1 — Reversing the subtraction order. You must keep y₂−y₁ and x₂−x₁ in the same order. If you compute (y₁−y₂)/(x₂−x₁), you'll flip the sign and get a wrong slope.

Mistake 2 — Dividing by zero. If x₁ = x₂ (a vertical line), slope is undefined — you cannot divide by 0. Vertical lines have no defined slope.

Mistake 3 — Confusing slope and intercept in word problems. The rate (per-unit change) is always the slope; the starting/base value is always the intercept. Misreading which is which is the most common real-world error.

Mistake 4 — Assuming steeper always means "bigger number." A slope of −10 is steeper than a slope of 3, even though −10 looks "smaller" as a number — compare absolute values (|m|) for steepness, not the raw sign.
