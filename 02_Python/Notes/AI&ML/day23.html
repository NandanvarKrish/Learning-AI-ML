What Is a Derivative?

~2 hours · The math behind how every ML model "learns"

01 Why this matters for AI/ML

When a model "learns" from data, what's actually happening is: an algorithm repeatedly asks "which direction should I nudge each parameter to reduce error?" — and the derivative is exactly what answers that question. Gradient descent, the algorithm that trains almost every ML model you'll build, is built entirely on derivatives. You don't need heavy calculus — just this one concept, deeply understood.

02 From slope of a line to slope of a curve

Recall Day 3: slope of a straight line = rise/run, and it's the same everywhere on that line. But most interesting real-world relationships aren't straight lines — they're curves, where the steepness changes at every point. A derivative answers: "what is the slope, right at this exact point on the curve?"

03 Building intuition: secant line → tangent line

Take f(x) = x². Pick a point, say x=2 (where f(2)=4). To estimate the slope there, first pick a nearby second point and draw a line between them (called a "secant line") — then keep bringing that second point closer and closer:

Second point xDistance from x=2Slope of secant line
31(9-4)/(3-2) = 5
2.50.5(6.25-4)/(2.5-2) = 4.5
2.10.1(4.41-4)/(2.1-2) = 4.1
2.010.01(4.0401-4)/(2.01-2) = 4.01

As the second point gets closer and closer to x=2, the slope keeps approaching exactly 4. That limiting value — the slope of the "tangent line" that just barely touches the curve at one point — IS the derivative at that point.

04 The power rule (the only formula you need today)

For a function f(x) = xⁿ, the derivative is:

f'(x) = n × x^(n-1)

Read "f'(x)" as "f prime of x" — it just means "the derivative of f."

f(x) = x²
f'(x) = 2x
f(x) = x³
f'(x) = 3x²
f(x) = 5x²
f'(x) = 10x
f(x) = 7 (constant)
f'(x) = 0
f(x) = 4x
f'(x) = 4
f(x) = x² + 3x
f'(x) = 2x + 3

Check the table example: f(x)=x² → f'(x)=2x → at x=2, f'(2) = 2(2) = 4. Matches exactly what the secant lines converged toward.

Why is the derivative of a constant always 0?

A constant function like f(x)=7 is a perfectly flat horizontal line — it never rises or falls no matter what x is. Slope of a flat line = 0, everywhere. This matches the power rule too: think of 7 as 7x⁰, and the rule gives 0×7x⁻¹ = 0.

05 Real-world example (marginal cost/profit)

Business/commerce example — you may recognize this term

A company's profit function based on units sold (x): Profit(x) = -2x² + 400x - 5000 (profit rises, then falls as overproduction increases costs — a realistic curve, not a straight line).

Derivative: Profit'(x) = -4x + 400

This derivative is exactly what economists call "marginal profit" — how much profit changes for one more unit sold, at any given production level. Setting it to 0 finds the point where profit stops increasing (the peak):

-4x + 400 = 0 → x = 100

At exactly 100 units, profit is at its maximum — sell fewer, you're leaving profit on the table; sell more, costs eat into profit. This exact technique — using the derivative to find where a function is at its best (or worst) point — is precisely how gradient descent finds a model's best parameters in Phase 3, just automated and repeated thousands of times.

06 Common mistakes to avoid

Mistake 1 — Confusing f(x) with f'(x). f(x) tells you the height/value of the curve; f'(x) tells you the slope at that point — two completely different pieces of information.

Mistake 2 — Forgetting the derivative of a constant is 0, not the constant itself. The derivative of "5" is 0, not 5.

Mistake 3 — Applying the power rule incorrectly. The exponent comes down as a multiplier, AND the exponent itself decreases by 1 — both steps are required, not just one.

Mistake 4 — Thinking the derivative is a single number for the whole function. Except for straight lines, the derivative is usually itself a function — it gives a different slope value depending on which x you plug in.
