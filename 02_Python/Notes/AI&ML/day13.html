Correlation vs. Causation

~2 hours · One of the most misunderstood ideas in all of data analysis

01 Why this matters for AI/ML

ML models are extremely good at finding correlations in data — patterns where two things move together. They are not designed to tell you whether one causes the other. Misreading a model's correlational findings as causal is one of the most common (and costly) mistakes made by beginners and professionals alike, especially when a model is used to justify a real business or policy decision.

02 What is correlation?

Correlation measures how strongly two variables move together, expressed as a correlation coefficient (r) ranging from -1 to +1:

-1
Perfect negative
0
No correlation
+1
Perfect positive

Positive correlation (r near +1): as one variable increases, the other tends to increase too. Example: hours studied and exam score.

Negative correlation (r near -1): as one increases, the other tends to decrease. Example: price of a product and quantity demanded.

No correlation (r near 0): no consistent relationship. Example: a person's shoe size and their favorite color.

03 What is causation?

Causation means one variable directly produces a change in another — not just moves alongside it. Establishing causation typically requires controlled experiments (like A/B tests) or careful statistical techniques designed specifically to rule out other explanations — it is NOT something you can conclude just by observing that two things are correlated.

04 The classic example (ice cream & drowning)

The textbook case, because it's so intuitive

Ice cream sales and drowning deaths are strongly positively correlated — months with higher ice cream sales also have more drowning incidents. Does ice cream cause drowning? Obviously not.

The real explanation: a confounding variable — hot weather. Hot weather causes both more ice cream buying AND more swimming (hence more drowning risk). The two variables are correlated only because they share a common cause, not because either causes the other.

05 Real-world example (business — the trap beginners fall into)

Business example

A company notices: months with higher ad spend also have higher sales. Correlation = 0.8 (strong). A junior analyst concludes: "more ad spend causes more sales — let's spend even more!"

But consider the confounders: Maybe the company increases ad spend seasonally (festival months), and sales rise in those months regardless of ads, just due to seasonal demand. Or maybe rising sales gave the company more budget to spend on ads (reverse causation — sales causing ad spend, not the other way around!). Correlation alone cannot distinguish between these explanations — you'd need a controlled experiment (e.g., randomly varying ad spend across similar regions) to know the true causal effect.

06 Second example (reverse causation)

A subtler trap — which direction does causation run?

A hospital notices: patients who stay longer in the hospital have worse health outcomes. Does staying longer cause worse outcomes? Almost certainly the reverse: patients who are already sicker require longer stays — sickness causes longer stays, not the other way around. Always ask: "could the arrow of causation point the opposite direction from what seems obvious?"

07 Common mistakes to avoid

Mistake 1 — "Correlated, therefore causal." The single most common statistical error in the world. Always ask what else could explain the pattern.

Mistake 2 — Ignoring confounding variables. Always ask: is there a third factor influencing both variables (like weather influencing both ice cream sales and drownings)?

Mistake 3 — Missing reverse causation. Just because A and B are correlated doesn't tell you whether A causes B, B causes A, or both influence each other.

Mistake 4 — Trusting an ML model's "feature importance" as causal proof. A model finding that a feature strongly predicts an outcome tells you it's a good predictor — not that changing that feature would actually change the outcome. This distinction matters enormously for real business/policy decisions built on model outputs.

For each scenario, identify: is this likely correlation without causation, and if so, what's the probable confounder or alternate explanation?
